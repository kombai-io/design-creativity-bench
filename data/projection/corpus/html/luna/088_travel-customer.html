How should we respond when a journey changes?

Configure a humane, auditable response for customers affected by a carrier-initiated schedule change on European rail journeys.

Draft · evidence complete

Give the customer room to choose before asking them to absorb the change.

When a rail operator moves a departure by more than 60 minutes, the customer should receive a plain-language explanation, two viable alternatives, and a no-fee refund route. The response is triggered by the published timetable delta, not by a customer having to discover the problem at the station. This makes the rule consistent across app, email, and assisted support. [1]

Configured behaviour: offer rebooking first, keep the original fare conditions, and hold the decision open for 72 hours after notification. If the new itinerary creates an overnight arrival or a missed protected connection, elevate the case to a human specialist rather than closing it automatically. [2]

Why this threshold and sequence

A 60-minute threshold catches material changes without turning minor platform adjustments into needless transactions. In the 2023 disruption sample, 71% of customers whose departure moved between 60 and 180 minutes selected an alternative when it was shown beside the original booking; only 19% chose a refund. Presenting choice in that order therefore protects continuity while keeping the customer in control. [3]

The 72-hour window is deliberately longer than the standard 24-hour response window because travellers often need to coordinate hotels, airport transfers, or companions. It does not waive the operator’s ticket conditions: it simply preserves the fare while the customer considers a documented alternative. After the window, the case remains eligible for manual review if the customer can show that the notice was not reasonably received. [4]

Boundaries for the service agent

Agents may approve one like-for-like rebooking across the same origin and destination, including a change of service within 24 hours of the affected departure. They must not imply that a hotel, meal, or onward flight is covered unless the disruption partner has confirmed that entitlement. Where a protected connection is lost, the agent records the missed segment and routes the customer to the disruption desk; the rule is designed to prevent a well-intentioned self-service exchange from silently breaking the rest of the journey. [5]

The intended outcome is continuity without pressure.

The customer sees what changed, what can be preserved, and what each choice means before confirming. A refund is never hidden behind a rebooking flow. The service language should acknowledge the practical impact of the change without promising compensation that has not been assessed.

Expected customer-facing sequence

First, notify the traveller using the booking language and show the old and new departure times. Second, place the nearest suitable alternative beside a clearly labelled refund option. Third, confirm the consequences of each choice, including any change to seat selection and connection protection. Finally, provide a case reference and keep the decision reversible within the configured window.

Measure of success: fewer customers repeat the same explanation to support, while refund and rebooking decisions remain traceable to the timetable event that caused them.
