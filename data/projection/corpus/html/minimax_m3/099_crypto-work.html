On the working register of people we keep meaning to print.

A working directory is not a network. It is not a CV database, not a recruiter's spreadsheet, and not, despite appearances, a public square. It is, in the most literal sense, an inventory — and like any inventory it has been put together by hand, by people who would rather be doing something else.

This is the third volume of the Register, and the first we have published as a standing document rather than a quarterly special. The directory you are reading catalogues just over four thousand working people across the cryptographic industry — researchers, auditors, operators, searchers, designers, lawyers, and the occasional full-time governance delegate — and we have spent the last nine months verifying, by hand, that each of them is still alive to the practice. About one in seven did not respond to our verification letters. We have removed them.

We do not say this as a complaint. We say it as a fact about an industry that has been asking its workers, for several years, to produce a great deal and to sleep somewhat less. The names below represent the people who, at the time of going to press, are still doing the work — at desks in Lagos and Lisbon, in co-working spaces in Buenos Aires and Brooklyn, in converted bank vaults in Mitte and shipping-container offices in Singapore. They are not, by and large, looking for work; they are doing it.

The Register is free, ad-free, and supported by three patrons whose names appear in the colophon. We do not run sponsored entries, we do not accept payment for inclusion, and we have, on three separate occasions, declined to list people who wished to be listed because we could not corroborate the work they claimed to be doing. If you have arrived here from a job posting, a recruitment agency, or a LinkedIn message requesting "ten senior zk engineers, please", please close the tab and read the methodology instead.

What follows is a selection of six entries from the December set. The full roster — searchable, filterable, exportable as CSV and as JSON-LD — lives at register.crypto and is updated, on a slow and deliberate cadence, every Friday at noon UTC.

Aleksandra Vorontsov

applied cryptographer · folding schemes
CityBerlin, DE Pronounsshe / her JoinedAug 2019 Verifiedby 3 collaborators

Aleksandra leads the proving-systems group at Geometry Labs, where she has spent the last eighteen months pushing forward folding-based arguments and pulling back on lookup gadgets. Her current obsession is reducing recursion overhead for prover-aggregated rollups; her team shipped the first non-trivial instance of Hyperplonk-over-lookup in production last August and has, she is careful to note, not yet shipped the second.

Before Geometry she spent four years at Aztec, where she helped deliver the recursive proof-of-circuits pipeline that powered Connect. She co-authored "Cairo under Plookup, Slowly", a paper that has been cited exactly seven times, six of them by people she has met at conferences. Outside of work she co-organises the Berlin zk-reading group and maintains a small library of constraint-generation primitives that nobody else uses but everyone she knows secretly depends on. She lives in a converted bank vault in Mitte with two cats and a Trezor that has never left its drawer.

Kenji Marlowe-Brent

protocol auditor · formal methods
CityLisbon, PT Pronounshe / him JoinedMar 2017 Verifiedby 4 collaborators

Kenji has been writing audit reports for nearly seven years and has lost count of the live networks that still rely on something he once shipped. He works out of a converted print-shop in Alfama and specialises in rollups and account abstraction; his last six engagements have all been 4337-related, and he has a particular fondness for fuzzing bundlers against adversarial mempools at three in the morning.

He maintains a private K-framework fork and contributes occasionally to the runtime verification team's reachability tooling. He has published one hundred and forty-two audit reports since 2017, including post-mortems on three exploits totalling $89M in losses, none of which he was responsible for and two of which he had, in some quiet way, warned about. He does not drink coffee after four in the afternoon. He does, however, drink gin, and he will tell you, if asked, what he thinks of ERC-7201.

Tomiwa Adesope

mev searcher · protocol engineer
CityLagos, NG Pronounshe / him JoinedApr 2020 Verifiedby 3 collaborators

Tomiwa runs a private searcher operation across Uniswap v4 hooks and the major intents protocols, with a particular focus on cross-domain arbitrage that resolves inside a single block. He has been profitable in every quarter since Q3 2021, which he claims is not because he is good but because the alternative would be embarrassing.

He also maintains a public mempool emulator used by a handful of other searchers and has contributed minor improvements to several AMM implementations. He is wary of token-incentive schemes and will not, on principle, touch a points program. When asked, last spring, whether he would consider running for a protocol council seat, he replied that he had considered it and decided against it, on the grounds that "councils are where searchers go to die". He is, despite the joke, one of the more publicly-readable operators in the Lagos cohort.

Yelena Piatigorsky

cryptographer · consensus & randomness
CityZürich, CH Pronounsshe / her JoinedJan 2015 Verifiedby 5 collaborators

Yelena works on consensus-layer primitives and unbiasable randomness, mostly in the context of proof-of-stake systems that are older than most of her colleagues. She spent eight years at the Ethereum Foundation working on validator behaviour and fork-choice, then took two years off to write a book on threshold cryptography that is, in her own words, "mostly wrong but useful".

She is now at a small foundation-funded lab in Zürich where she is working on a VDF construction that nobody has yet managed to break. She refuses to use Twitter, has never had a Discord account, and maintains, in lieu of either, a low-traffic mailing list on her own domain that about nine hundred people actually read. She is the only person in this volume whose entry we did not need to follow up on.

How the directory is made

Entries are accepted on the basis of three independent confirmations, at least one of which must be a working collaborator of the applicant. We do not charge for inclusion. We do, however, ask applicants to attest in writing that the work they describe is work they have actually done.

The verification letters go out by post to a randomly sampled 5% of entrants, and by email to the remainder. Letters are signed by hand. They take, on average, eleven days to receive a response, and we have, in the last volume, had to chase people who took longer than that. We will not name them.

Names, pronouns, ENS handles, and primary addresses are public. Phone numbers, residential addresses, employer-of-record, and current salary bands are not held in the directory at all and have never been, contrary to persistent rumour.

About this volume

Set in Iowan Old Style for prose and Inter for the apparatus, with JetBrains Mono for all cryptographic identifiers. Pagination is constructed in CSS Grid; entries are formatted in a single column at fifty-eight characters. The page is set for a 1280px desktop canvas. Mobile rendering is, by editorial decision, not supported.

Volume III was edited by M. Halberstam and R. Okafor, with research assistance from seven volunteer reviewers whose names appear on the errata page. The masthead glyph is a stylised volvelle, because we thought it appropriate.
